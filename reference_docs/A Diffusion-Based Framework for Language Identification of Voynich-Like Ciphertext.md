In this paper, we focus on determining the most probable language that is encoded in the Voynich manuscript text. We improve upon recent transformer-based plaintext language identification and decipherment models by extending their ability to handle a more complicated class of substitution ciphers. Specifically, we develop models capable of handling advanced ciphers capable of generating cipher text similar to that of the Voynich manuscript.   
  
Plaintext language identification via trial decipherment has been used by the automated decryption community for decades. The method is based on the idea that partially decrypted text can still carry language identifying characteristics. Early implementations such as the Noisy-Channel EM method (Knight 2006) and the Tree Search n-gram method (Hauer 2016) were generally brute force "guess and check" based procedures. Although useful, these methods had a tendency to be brittle and were difficult to scale to larger numbers of languages.   
  
Recently, end-to-end neural methods such as the decoder only transformer with recurrence encoding (Kambhatla, 2023) have addressed some of the issues of the previous generation and have begun to dominate the field. These methods have shown impressive results on 1:1 substitution and unigram homophonic ciphers, but have not yet been adapted tto the more complicated cipher classes that are more plausible candidates for the Voynich text.  
  
In this paper, we develop end-to-end neural methods capable of handling the more complicated cipher classes required to generate the enciphered text with the statistical signatures of the Voynich text. Our primary contributions are:  
  
1) Developing decoding head architectures capable of handling mixed unigram-bigram homophonic ciphers and arithmetic encoded homophonic ciphers.   
2) Using a multi-lingual text diffusion model backbone with a jointly trained language detection head resulting in improved robustness to noise and availability of the evidence lower bound (ELBO) as a language metric.  
  
In all experiments, whitespace is removed from every text stream — training corpora, synthetic ciphertexts, and the Voynich transcription itself — as an early preprocessing step. All models therefore operate on unsegmented character sequences, and word-boundary information plays no role in the reported rankings. We validate the method using historical corpora enciphered with standard classical systems, and two recently proposed cipher systems explicitly tuned to reproduce the Voynich Manuscript's statistical signatures. The first is the Naibbe cipher (Greshko 2025), a hand-executable verbose homophonic substitution that has has been shown to encipher Latin and Italian into Voynich-like text. The second is a sum-to-target arithmetic homophonic cipher — the pseudo-Voynich generator from Boxer's voynich-attack project (github.com/alexanderboxer/voynich-attack) — in which each plaintext letter is enciphered as one variable-length token whose cipher-character values sum to the letter's assigned integer, with homophone sampling and token-doubling rates tuned to reproduce Voynich token statistics across multiple candidate languages.  
  
We then apply the framework to the Voynich Manuscript and rank candidate cipher systems and plaintext languages. We emphasize that these rankings are exploratory and assumption-dependent rather than a claimed solution. Among the languages tested, Germanic candidates receive the highest likelihood under our current framework. Results for the cipher system are less definitive: the complex decoding heads outscore the simple homophonic heads, but no single complex head clearly dominates.  
  
We close by noting limitations and discussing future work including the working the treatment of word-boundary and scribal phenomena specific to the Voynich text. The repository containing MIT licensed code and models will be made available upon publication.
